# Sejr arcana: long assessment

Companion to *Sejr Arcana Assessment.md*, which stays as the summary. Deck of record: *Sejr Arcana Deck.md* (rule book appendix A, 39 cards). The Perchance module is ignored. Rules ambiguities are left to the October audit, so the card notes below judge design and fit, not wording.

**The two lenses.** The essay lens holds the deck up against the Kobold library and the GM's Toolkit on magic items, rewards, drop-in episodic play and consequence design. The mechanism lens treats arcana as the campaign's progression spine (unexamined object, ritual, resource, power, escalation, absent ticks, University ranks) and tests it against a drop-in, one-gig, three-hour, thirteen-session campaign. Your own tests run through both: Costs, Connects, Clean; Ockham; the armature; the Spread test.

**Invention.** Nothing here adds facts about the campaign or the players. Anything that leans past the rule book is marked *speculation*.

---

## Lens 1: the essays

**Thesis.** On cost, meaning and consequence the deck is close to a model answer to the Kobold essays. It parts company with them on precision and on who authors the object.

### What the deck gets right

- **Cost.** Rosenberg (*Cost Determines Value*, Guide to Magic) says free magic "loses its luster", and that the best costs are unique to the wielder, "an expression and concentration of their soul". Scholes (*Making Magic Believable*) adds that cost and limits open "opportunities to use the drawbacks of magic as a part of the story". Sejr's costs are rarely gold. They are a memory (Unpaid Favor), your name for a week, an eye's colour (Fairy Jar), max HP on the record (Oath-Band), a Wound you don't remember (Knucklebone). Every Risk line is a story cost first and a mechanical one second.
- **Meaning.** Chart (*More Than a Tool*) sorts ways to keep magic from feeling like technology. The two that fit Sejr are "ethical" and "meaningful" magic, where the effect follows the significance of the act. Refill-by-deed is meaningful magic as a resource rule: apologise for a real wrong (Apology Blade), bury someone with no one to bury them (Binding Linens), return a relic to its owner (Ledger), teach a craft (Codex), retell a death to a new listener (Knucklebone). Chart also lists "ancient and fixed" magic, where the item works only on its own terms, "no matter how inconvenient". That describes the gig-bound refills: the Mud wants the Marshes, the Spear wants Bloodrust Depths.
- **People as consequence.** Winter (*Sharpening Your Hooks*, Plots and Campaigns) says "make it about people, not things", and recommends hooks built on past mistakes. Nearly every Escalation sends a person, and most of those people are tied to a faction already in the book. Greenwood (*The Quest for Great Lost Magic*) describes old items whose use wakes hunters sent to "return the objects to their proper storage". That is the Bear-Lodge Horn, the Axe of Nine Eyes and the Rusted Spear.
- **An opposing position with a case.** Many claimants are in the right: the lodge, the valkyrie, the fey who were promised freedom, the rightful owners in the Ledger, the Wardens who want the chain back on the gate, the card sharp whose die was taken from an evidence shelf. Read together, the deck makes one recurring argument about who owns a thing that came through an arch. *Speculation, not checked against your armature:* if the campaign's point touches route politics or ownership, the deck is already the visible counter-case, and it is still standing at the end, which your armature rule demands.
- **Present-tense deeds.** Stafford (*Take Your Character on a Transformative Journey*, Guide to Roleplaying) warns against characters "beholden to the immutable events of the past" and asks for choices made now. The best unlocks are exactly that: refuse a just verdict to protect someone you love (Broken Verdict), heal an enemy with healing an ally needed (Bell), swear something you would not otherwise do (Oath-Band), tell an NPC the moment you would undo (Hourglass).

### Where the deck pulls against the essays

- **Precision.** Cook (*Impractical Magic*) writes that "a system is the enemy of wonder", and wants players making "judgments, not dictates". Chart warns that one magic system per character "is a lot of work". Sejr gives every hero their own subsystem, and the five d6-against-accumulation cards do not even share a timing rule. Feather rolls after adding. Harpoon, Setlist and Cord roll before. The Lens rolls first and only adds on a safe roll. Ockham's test: the variation adds nothing to play, so shave it to one procedure.
- **Fetch deeds.** Stafford's point cuts the other way on unlocks that are errands: find the workshop (Kettle Plate), pay the pawn shop (Lens), find a second fragment (Rune), dig it from the grave (Feather). They are fine as leads. They are weak as the final, at-the-table deed, which the rule book saves for the ritual's climax.
- **Who authors the object.** Winter advises letting the new owner of a found weapon "provide details for it", so later hooks land on something they invested in. Grubb (*Colors of Magic*) argues for player-made "skins" of magic. Sejr goes the other way. "Don't read the card yet" keeps the mystery with the GM, and the object line is written in full. That is a deliberate trade, and it buys the unexamined phase. It costs the player a hand in the thing they carry for thirteen sessions.
- **Hoarding.** Greenwood (*Quest for Great Lost Magic*) warns that scarce magic swaps excitement for "the anxiety of resource management", and says "the trick is to keep the resource part of things, yet not allow it to overwhelm the excitement". Three cards are finite (Sown Teeth, Axe, Seven-Leaf), and a dozen more refill only off-city. In drop-in play, where a player may not know when they will next attend, scarce charges tend to sit unused.
- **Omens.** Winter also warns not to "overwork the omens", suggesting one every five significant missions. Five heroes with Escalations that each send someone can produce more visitors than the thread board can hold. The rule book already routes overflow into the closest clock, which is the right fix. Watch that visitors don't become the default texture of every session.

### Reward cadence

Nesmith (*Zen and the Art of Loot Distribution*, Guide to Dungeons) separates story campaigns, where loot should be "objects with histories", from optimiser campaigns, where "at every level, somebody is getting something". Sejr is firmly the first kind, and needs none of his level-by-level tables. His cadence point still applies, though. The steady drip in Sejr is the ritual, and it stops at waking (see Lens 2).

### Counterargument

The library assumes a standing group and a GM steering one story. Baur (*Choosing an Ending First*) even calls episodic play "picaresque", prone to "static, sitcom-style relationships". Sejr is an open-table design that these books barely address. Cook's case against precision also cuts against Nimble, which is a tactical grid game. Full numbers on the card may be the right price for a card a drop-in player can pick up cold, with no GM memory needed.

## Lens 2: arcana as the core mechanism

**Thesis.** The loop is a strong spine from draw to waking. After waking, most cards become upkeep, so the campaign's longest act has nothing for the arcanum to aim at.

### Phase by phase

- **Draw and unexamined (strong).** The draw gives every hero a mystery from session one. Clue rolls, the Beard table and suffered traits feed it. This is your Spark: planted, and unread by the player.
- **Ritual (strong, and short).** Two or three deeds. Absence ticks one, suffering a trait ticks one, and only the final deed has to happen at the table. A two-box ritual can fill in two or three sessions. That suits Smoulder. It is also over by about session four of thirteen.
- **Awake: resource and powers (works).** Refill-by-deed keeps the object in the fiction, and off-city refills turn into gigs. The rule book calls them "a ready-made hero stake", which passes Connects, and Boomer's episodic model (*When Last We Left Our Intrepid Heroes*) is satisfied: each episode "a cohesive mini-tale" plus one move.
- **University ranks (empty).** Novice and Reader track the ritual. Wielder ("reliable use") and Cracker ("broken open") have no content on any card. Nothing on a card says what breaking it open means.
- **Absence after waking (empty).** The promise that absent heroes "tick an arcana ritual instead of losing ground" runs out once the boxes are full. After that, absence yields one off-screen refill, the same as attending.

### The Spread test

Asked of arcana in the middle act: can the player name what they are trying to get? Before waking, yes: wake it. After waking, the honest answer for most cards is "keep it topped up". Upkeep never moves anyone closer or further, so two hollow sessions in a row are likely for any hero whose card has no second stage. Chart predicts this: found magic stops feeling magical after a few sessions and becomes "nothing more than a bonus on die rolls".

### Which cards have a second stage

About ten have an end state past waking: Black Lotus (Stain 3, the bloom roots), Apology Blade (the storm wakes), Rune Fragment (the ninth fuses), Oath-Band (graded oaths, max HP capped by level), Fairy Jar (smash it, owe the fey), Seven-Leaf (it buds), Axe of Nine Eyes (the ninth eye, your True Name spoken), Knucklebone (it cracks and is replaced), Hollow Contract (sentences pile up), Codex (it moves on). Greenwood's Rod of Seven Parts model is the template: parts, rivals, an assembled whole. These are the cards that already have a Cracker. Baur's ending-first advice and your step seven point the same way. Each hero's arcanum could have a designed last beat.

### Table size

- **One player.** Close to ideal. Greenwood (*Feasts and Famines*, Guide to Gamemastering) describes a one-on-one campaign built on "tracking down old tomes" and lore "while dealing with the sinister scrutiny of foes". That is the Sejr ritual. Solo math still needs checking on the combat-heavy cards (Hellhide, Sown Teeth, Battle Standard), because solo survivability does not use party benchmarks.
- **Full table.** One gig per session. Five heroes with gig-only refills compete for about thirteen gigs, so each arcanum gets two or three episodes where it is the stake. Greenwood warns that splitting a big table's spotlight leaves a subset "out of the limelight". Winter's counsel helps: "a strong appeal to a few characters' stories can be better than a weak appeal to many", if players trust everyone gets a turn. *Speculation:* one gig that refills two heroes' cards at once (the Marshes for Mud and Tide-Coat, a world through an arch for Horn and Fairy Jar) would ease the queue.

### Costs, Connects, Clean, deck-wide

- **Costs: pass.** Every card costs the bearer something they feel.
- **Connects: pass, strongly.** Cards name factions, places and NPCs already in the book, and some feed the trackers directly (Mud's Neglect clock, the Ledger's Black Hand clock, the Forgotten Key's arches).
- **Clean: fail at deck level.** Five resource types, five d6 timings, per-card DCs, and GM-held secrets for every hero. Each card is clean alone. Five at one table is not.

### Counterargument

The arcanum may not be meant to carry the middle. The thread board, the patrons' stage tracks and the key-making front (Find, Read, Make) are built for that job, and arcana may be the personal B-plot that feeds them through Escalations. If so, the plateau matters less. The University ranks are still dead labels, and absent players still have nothing to tick after session four.

## Card by card

Each note gives a verdict on how well the card carries the loop (strong, mid, weak), then what drives that.

### Found arcana

**1. The Drowning Scale** (mid). Strong unlock: a lunar cycle passes off-screen and suits drop-in play, and the near-drowning is a scene. Each night's honest answer hands the GM a player-sourced secret, which is the deck's best Connects device for free. Breath refills only in combat, so the card lives in fights. No end state.

**2. The Hourglass of Borrowed Time** (strong). The unlock is a confession, a present-tense deed. Turn Back on any visible die is a big power for 3 Sand, and the refill sends you back to the place you last used it. The Echo is the deck's best consequence design: the result you erased arrives as a person who wants the glass. The d6 table is a ready episode. No end state, but the Echo is a recurring engine.

**3. The Black Lotus Case** (strong). Three deeds, one of them a lead (find a survivor). The Stain is visible, permanent progression with a defined end, and the end changes how you hold the item. The refill (seed where someone you knew died) is meaningful and can be done in the city. A model card for the middle act.

**4. The Oath-Band** (strong, best in deck). The player writes the stake, and a Worthy oath is "a session's work", which fits one gig per session exactly. Fulfilment adds max HP for good. Failure goes on the Golden Council's record under your name. It is the one card that generates its own Spread-test answer every session.

**5. The Unpaid Favor** (mid). Call In is open-ended and GM-approved, the closest the deck gets to Cook's wonder. That is also its GM load. Tithe clears only by a favour the GM presents, so the card writes hooks. The collection table mixes comedy (Newt) with long consequence (Nemesis). Fine for a city of genres. No end state.

**6. The Valkyrie's Feather** (strong). The unlock is a fetch plus a story, the weaker half. Spared is a strong, simple reaction. The Escalation is the deck's armature in miniature: the valkyrie "will hear one argument", so the opposing position gets a voice and a vote. The refill is a gig beyond an arch.

**7. The Hellhide** (mid). A heavy Major. The 1 to 3 Risk blocks healing magic, including allies' healing, which costs the whole table. Thirst clears only with willing blood from outside the party, outside combat. That is dark, meaningful, and a scene. Terror and Command are combat-only, and Terror's per-creature tracking adds load. The spectre ending is strong. Check solo math.

**8. The Kettle Plate** (weak). One vendor, one fetch, one price refills it. The powers are flat numbers and a free attack. Chart would call it technology, and it reads as technology in the fiction too. Vathrex's Forge and Erna Ironhorn give it Connects. Nothing else carries the loop.

**9. The Fairy Jar** (strong). The name has to be learned through the arch, and each refill is a question about the fairy's home whose answer lies through an arch. That makes the card a clue engine for arch gigs. Smashing it is a real end state with a cost (a stat for good, a fey favour owed). The 2d6-day sulk passes off-screen cleanly.

**10. The Apology Blade** (strong). Two-stage wake. The refill is a social deed about a real wrong. Overcast is a reading power with a conscience. The Escalation keeps the person you wronged in play. Every step is a present-tense choice, in Stafford's sense.

**11. The Rune Fragment** (strong, with a size risk). A collection quest with rivals who follow the call, and a relight that asks for the name of someone who died for something. The ninth fragment is a designed final beat. The risk is scale: nine fragments is a lot for one hero in thirteen shared sessions. *Speculation:* fewer fragments, or fragments that turn up in other heroes' gigs, would fit the budget.

**12. The Binding Linens** (mid). The unlock and refill are both the same meaningful deed, burying the unclaimed, and it can be done in the city. Hold Back is niche. The second-wrap Escalation (it forgets one thing it loved) is a fine cost and rarely triggers. No end state.

**13. The Rusted Spear** (mid to strong). The unlock is restitution: find whom it last wounded and heal them. Spare changes lethality, which gives meaning to every kill. The refill is a Bloodrust Depths gig. The clean-blade Escalation is a strong threat. No end state past waking.

**14. The Derelict Harpoon** (mid). A good combat toy with a clear risk curve. The clear (splice the line back in Drendaan) is a gig, and the follower through the arch is a hook. It has little to do with meaning or the University, and no end state.

**15. The Seekers' Provenance Ledger** (strong). The refill is the deck's theme in one act: return a relic, and have the owner sign. The Black Hand clock starts at unlock. The Escalation writes a relic belonging to someone you know, and both factions learn it. The d6 includes "an arcanum another hero carries", which ties heroes together.

**16. The Black Goat's Tally-Stick** (mid to strong). Brokering a peace is a superb deed for a city of patrons. The Truce resets only after another brokered meeting, which is gig-sized and social. Read the Notches is a reading power, covered by the rule book's Readings rule. Vynan Halfhorn is a natural returning NPC. No end state.

**17. The Gate Chain** (mid to strong). Ward refills by rescuing someone who cannot get out alone, which happens on many gigs without special setup. That makes it the most drop-in-friendly refill in the deck. The tank kit is clear. The Wardens' Lodge wants it back on the gate, which ties it to the Hunter guild. No end state.

**18. The Sown Teeth** (weak, worst in deck). Finite, with no refill and no arc, and it ends as "just a bag". The minion rules are solid, and the friendly-fire Escalation is a tactics glitch where every other card sends a person. Greenwood's hoarding warning fits it exactly.

**19. The Hut on Hen's Legs** (mid to strong). A holding that walks is a wonderful Major, and Run and Stamp are big. Hunger refills with food from beyond the city, which nudges gigs outward. The Sisters of Dusk tie it to a faction. The loop risk is drop-in play: the hut walks off if no one sleeps in it for an episode, so an absent owner's party has to remember it.

**20. The Crafter's Codex** (mid). Its life is downtime: Consult, the teaching refill, Maker's Price. That is good for absent play and invisible at the table. Moving on at 0 Patience is a soft end state, and the Forge's smiths are a ready hook.

### Legacy draws

**21. The Hexing Needles** (mid to strong). Ethical magic in Chart's sense: the victim feels every stitch, and the material must be given knowing what it is for. The refill qualifies for off-screen. At 0 Thread the hex lands on you, a clean, personal Escalation. The Sisters of Dusk tie it in.

**22. The Bear-Lodge Horn** (strong). The unlock is an arc in itself: through the arch, bring a hunt, blow it in their hall. The refill is a report to the lodge, so use is judged by its owners. Naming the speaker at the table shares authorship. Heavy travel suits a legacy draw.

**23. The Axe of Nine Eyes** (strong). Finite, with a designed ending: the ninth eye closes and the axe speaks your True Name. Named creatures learn your heading each dawn, so every use costs you something lasting. The Fallen Angels and Graydust Keep give it a claimant.

### Faction rewards

**24. The Forgotten Key** (strong, underrated in the short ranking). It is the card closest to the campaign's premise, since keys decide routes. Hum refills only by trying the key on an untried arch during a gig, with an Archkeeper recording the result. That makes exploration the refill. It feeds the key-making front directly. *Speculation:* this card could anchor the front's Read stage.

**25. The Tide-Coat** (weak). Growth comes from hours underwater "beyond what the gig needs", which is a chore, and it drains for episodes without diving. The Defense and Stealth trade is neat. Its only end state is becoming a landmark.

**26. The Pitch-Sap Harness** (weak). Burden is a combat penalty with no deed attached. The sealing Escalation is a good image. The refill (fresh sap from the tree that gave it) is the only link to the loop, and it only fixes damage.

**27. The Seven-Leaf Bracelet** (mid to strong). Finite, with a cycle: it buds, you plant it by a door to elsewhere, and a new one grows. The Risk, where every plant in the grove knows your secret, is excellent. Graysen One-Eye is a ready claimant.

**28. The Arch-Watch Mirror** (strong for drop-in). It serves the whole party (Foresee before an arch), refills with a vigil that qualifies for off-screen, and installs in a holding or guild hall, so it keeps working while its owner is absent. The both-ways Escalation is a fine horror beat.

### Class arcana

**29. The Ancestor's Knucklebone** (strong). The refill is a retelling to a new listener, which pushes roleplay into the room. Death's Door is a dramatic one-shot, and the cracked bone sends you to a replacement table: an end state that starts a new arc.

**30. The Loaded Die** (mid). Fix is flat (roll twice, keep one). The refill (an honest game during a Safe Rest) qualifies for off-screen. The hidden Escalation roll is good tension. The card sharp is a strong claimant. No end state.

**31. The Battle Standard of the Nameless King** (weak). Authority is encounter-bound, so nothing carries between sessions. The unlock needs five NPC combatants, which is hard in drop-in or solo play. The insignia d6 is the card's best feature, and it is locked behind a narrow sequence. It has the strongest lore hook of any class card.

**32. The Borderlands Tracking Cord** (mid). A one-box ritual that needs two consecutive sessions of tracking the same quarry. That is the hardest unlock in the deck for a drop-in player who misses a week. The Mark economy and the hunter-hunted Escalation are well built.

**33. The Cracked Lens of Kartan** (mid to weak). The unlock is heavy: a price, a week, a check, and a top-tier cast. The safe-roll powers branch three ways by subclass, which is the clearest Clean failure among the class cards. The Mages of Kartan ties it to the University, which is the card's best Connects.

**34. The Broken Verdict** (strong). The best unlock in the deck: refuse a just verdict to protect someone you love. The lie-tilt that only you see is a reading power with a dilemma attached ("You must decide what to do with that"). The fusion Escalation sends you to the temple with questions. No end state.

**35. The Hollow Contract** (strong). The Escalation is the reward. Each collection writes a permanent sentence about your character, read aloud. The blood-sentence refill is a vow the player writes. Two paths cover both subclasses.

**36. The Bell of the Three Sisters** (mid to strong). A strong unlock (heal an enemy with healing an ally needed). The refill (an hour over a sickbed or grave) qualifies for off-screen and ties to the Shepherd guild's ask. Toll cracks the handle, a small, clear end-of-use beat.

**37. The Theater of Whispers Setlist** (mid). The unlock is a performance hour, which is easy to stage. Crowd clears with a new audience of 20 or more, a city scene. The "who knows the setlist" d6 is a good hook table. The in-combat Call rules add per-round rolling.

**38. Mud from the Revlend Marshes** (strong). The refill is an overnight in the Marshes, and each return shows the Marshes changed, including revealing a Neglect step. It is the card most wired into the trackers. Every refill is also a report on a neglected place.

**39. The Pain Teacher's Wrappings** (weak). The Wound cost is honest and thematic, but nothing refills by deed, so the card never touches the ritual or refill loop. The re-inking hook is its only route back into play.

---

## Ranked

**Best for the loop:** Oath-Band, Rune Fragment, Apology Blade, Black Lotus Case, Hollow Contract, Provenance Ledger, then Forgotten Key, Broken Verdict, Mud, Arch-Watch Mirror.

**Worst:** Sown Teeth, Battle Standard, Pain Teacher's Wrappings, Kettle Plate, Pitch-Sap Harness, Tide-Coat.

**Patterns behind the ranking.** The strong cards share three things: a refill that is a deed in the fiction, a claimant with a case, and an end state past waking. The weak cards are missing at least two of them, usually by refilling through combat or chores.

## Proposed next steps (not done)

- **A Cracker line for each card.** One sentence naming each card's end state past waking. Key Wielder and Cracker to card events, and let post-wake absent ticks move toward it. This closes the middle-act gap.
- **One d6-against-accumulation procedure** for all five cards.
- **Shared refill gigs.** Pair cards whose refills point to the same region, so one gig can carry two heroes.
